INTRODUCTION 
AVANT will deliver an integrated SW solution to build and deploy Digital Twins articulated in 8 specific Software platforms, to 
be realized in 8 WPs, all with RDI and FDI phases. The first 3 WPs will deliver enablers fundamental to the effective delivery of 
Digital Twins, by supporting secure and trusted access, management and optimal exploitation of distributed resources, data, 
and services. On top of them, AVANT will deliver 5 domain-specific Digital Twins platforms for Manufacturing, Cultural 
Heritage, Urban services, Energy and Healthcare. 
The innovations proposed are a) creating a flexible interoperability layer, foundation for advanced computing (WP1); b) 
leveraging WP1 to create a data continuum considering data produced at edge/far-edge (WP2); c) secure Digital Twins in 
collaborative cyber-physical environments and apply Digital Twins for security (WP3); d) enable modular, low-cost, flexible 
and scalable Digital Twins in manufacturing, (WP4); e) use Digital Twins and AI for cultural heritage (CH) conservation, 
preservation, restoration, and fruition (WP5); f) UDT for an effective multi-level data-driven governance, for territorial long-
term resilience and sustainability strategies (WP6); g) exploit Digital Twins to enable improved operations in decentralized 
energy systems(WP7); h) provide a comprehensive platform for evidence-based medicine (WP8). AVANT will overcome 
different locks: heterogeneous cloud services, poor orchestration; different data formats, data distribution, lack of holistic 
protection for Digital Twins; insufficient interoperability of connected devices; overlapping standards, presence of tech silos. 
AVANT will last 4.5 years - 3 years of RDI and 1.5 years of FID. 
In the digital transformation era, digital twins have become essential across various industries, including manufacturing, 
construction, healthcare, and urban planning. These virtual replicas of physical objects, processes, systems, or environments 
enable enhanced data analysis and system monitoring, leading to better decision-making. However, despite their vast 
potential, digital twins pose significant ethical and legal concerns that businesses and regulators must address carefully. 
A digital twin is essentially a dynamic digital representation of a physical entity or system, built using real-time data from 
sensors and other data-gathering instruments attached to its physical counterpart. This model can simulate, predict, and 
optimize the performance and maintenance of physical entities before actual procedures are implemented. In fields like 
Building Information Modelling (BIM) and geospatial systems, digital twins are invaluable for creating more efficient and 
sustainable solutions. Yet, as the technology spreads across sectors, the granularity and sheer volume of data involved 
present profound legal and ethical challenges. 
Digital twins depend on a continuous influx of detailed, often sensitive data from various sources, including IoT devices, which 
can include personal and proprietary information. This data might encompass operational characteristics of machinery, 
personal health records in healthcare, or real-time environmental data from smart city infrastructures. The detailed simulation 
and prediction capabilities of digital twins necessitate collecting and processing data at a scale and depth that inherently 
increases the risk of privacy breaches. 
Ethical and societal issues are integral to our use of technology. As technology becomes increasingly pervasive, affecting 
every aspect of life, these issues become more urgent and important. In the realm of digital twins, ethical and societal issues 
are particularly critical since the technology directly impacts human beings. Digital twins connect physical entities to their 
mirror images in cyberspace, allowing tasks to be performed in cyberspace without the physical entities, increasing efficiency 
and flexibility. Over time, AI functions that utilize digital twins (data analytics, simulation, etc.) have become embedded into 
the digital twin itself. This evolution is changing the definition and concept of a digital twin—no longer just a digital replica of 
a physical entity, it now includes enhanced functionality (intelligence) not present in the physical entity. Therefore, the ethical 
pillars for trustworthy AI must also be embedded into the digital twin. 
From an ethical and legal perspective, the use and deployment of digital twins raise questions about data generation, use, 
processing, and storage. The first legal question concerns the data sources for digital twins, particularly whether the data is 
obtained from internal or external data sets. In the more challenging case of external data mining, trustworthy data spaces 
that facilitate data access and allow for individual agreements are required. For digital twin applications affecting software 
source code, appropriate rights of use must be granted. If data sets processed allow conclusions to be drawn about natural

ETHICAL FRAMEWORK IN AVANT 
The importance of handling the AVANT project from an ethical perspective, particularly over the next three years, cannot be 
overstated. Upholding the highest EU standards and values is crucial. Given the project’s complexity, it is sensible to examine 
its activities through the lens of research ethics. This approach will help define the AVANT research policy and address the 
ethical challenges that may arise. 
Research ethics encompass a wide variety of values, norms, and institutional arrangements that help constitute and regulate 
scientific activities. Guidelines for research ethics specify the basic norms and values of the research community, based on the 
general ethics of science, which in turn are rooted in the morality of society at large. 
These guidelines primarily cover research but also extend to other research-related activities such as teaching, dissemination 
of research, expert advice, and institutional management. They apply to all public and private research, whether basic, 
applied, or commissioned. They also govern activities at consulting firms to the extent that these firms perform research-
related tasks, such as the systematic acquisition and processing of information about persons, groups, or organizations to 
develop new knowledge on specific matters. 
The guidelines are based on recognized norms for research ethics, regulating research in various areas and relationships: 
 
Norms that constitute good scientific practice, related to the quest for accurate, adequate, and relevant knowledge 
(academic freedom, originality, openness, trustworthiness, etc.). 
 
Norms that regulate the research community (integrity, accountability, impartiality, criticism, etc.). 
 
The relationship to people who take part in the research (respect, human dignity, confidentiality, free and informed 
consent, etc.). 
 
The relationship to the rest of society (independence, conflicts of interest, social responsibility, dissemination of 
research, etc.). 
The first two groups of ethical norms are internal, linked to the self-regulation of the research community, while the latter two 
groups are external, linked to the relationship between research and society. 
In the following sub-sections, we will first examine the ethical challenges associated with the development and use of Digital 
Twins, acknowledging that this list is not exhaustive. Based on these initial challenges, we will define the principles of good 
research practices embedded in the AVANT ethical framework and explore compliance with human rights. 
2.1 
ETHICAL CHALLENGES IN AVANT 
Digital Twins represent virtual replicas of physical entities, systems, or processes. These virtual models, continuously updated 
with data from their physical counterparts, enable real-time simulations, analysis, and optimization. Industries ranging from 
manufacturing to healthcare and urban planning have begun to harness the power of Digital Twins to predict future 
conditions, optimize operations, and improve decision-making processes. 
However, the innovative potential of Digital Twins should not overshadow the importance of the legal framework. The 
development of Digital Twins involves addressing certain legal obstacles and clarifying the applicable laws. Digital Twins are 
based on contextualized data, which is transformed into actionable information. At the heart of Digital Twins are data, 
algorithms, and AI systems. Therefore, the ethical and legal framework surrounding these concepts is crucial for the 
development of Digital Twins.

From an ethical and legal standpoint, Digital Twins raise several important questions regarding data privacy, liability, 
algorithmic integrity, and intellectual property. This list is far from exhaustive. While the most relevant legislations will be 
analyzed in Section 3, the following paragraphs will focus on the ethical challenges of Digital Twins and the AVANT project. 
Privacy and Data Protection 
One of the foremost ethical concerns surrounding Digital Twins is the issue of privacy and data protection. Digital Twins often 
require the collection, processing, and storage of vast amounts of data, some of which may be highly sensitive or personal. In 
healthcare, for example, a Digital Twin of a patient might include detailed medical histories, genetic information, and real-
time health data. Without stringent data protection measures, there is a risk of unauthorized access and misuse of this 
information, potentially leading to privacy violations and discrimination. 
Ensuring the security of the data involved in Digital Twins is paramount. Organizations must adopt robust data protection 
protocols and comply with relevant data protection regulations, such as the General Data Protection Regulation in the 
European Union. Furthermore, transparency regarding data collection, usage, and storage policies can help build trust and 
reassure individuals about the safety of their information. 
Intellectual Property and Ownership Issues 
Digital Twins also pose complex questions regarding intellectual property (IP) and ownership. The creation of a Digital Twin 
involves various components—software, algorithms, data, and the physical entity itself. Determining who owns the Digital 
Twin and the data it generates can be challenging, especially when multiple stakeholders are involved. For instance, does the 
manufacturer of a machine own the Digital Twin, or does ownership belong to the customer who uses the machine and 
generates the data?  
Clarifying IP rights and ownership early in the development process is crucial to avoid disputes and ensure that all parties 
benefit fairly from the technology. Agreements should specify the rights to use, modify, and distribute the Digital Twin and 
the data it generates. Additionally, considering licensing models could provide a flexible framework for the sharing and 
utilization of Digital Twins across different stakeholders. 
Liability 
The scope of liability is under great pressure from the players in the digital world. Connected objects, robots and autonomous 
devices are upsetting the classic rules of civil and criminal liability. It is necessary to adapt a legal framework that has been 
thought up to now for people with will and independence because liability is generally the counterpart of a right or a duty. 
Up to now, it has been assumed that machines were not able to act by themselves, but they have bridged the gap and 
become autonomous in the new virtual world. Is the profile the person? Can the real person be held responsible for damage 
caused by his or her digital twin? The reflection is old and can feed into already developed examinations about the liability of 
robots. Who is responsible: the programmer, the manufacturer, the owner of the robot or the machine?  
Ethical Use and Misuse 
The potential misuse of Digital Twins for unethical purposes presents another significant concern. For instance, in the realm of 
cybersecurity, attackers could exploit Digital Twins to simulate and plan attacks on physical systems, leading to security 
breaches, sabotage, or even physical harm. Similarly, in the context of employment, companies could use Digital Twins to 
monitor and analyze employee behavior excessively, raising issues of surveillance and autonomy. 
To mitigate these risks, it is essential to establish ethical guidelines and regulations governing the use of Digital Twins. These 
guidelines should emphasize the importance of consent, respect for individual autonomy, and the prohibition of exploitative

Social and Environmental Impacts 
Finally, the widespread adoption of Digital Twins may have significant social and environmental impacts. On one hand, Digital 
Twins can contribute to sustainability by optimizing resource use and reducing waste. On the other hand, there are concerns 
about the digital divide and unequal access to the benefits of this technology. As Digital Twins become increasingly

the necessary technologies and skills. 
Addressing these social and environmental considerations requires a holistic approach. Policies and initiatives to promote 
digital literacy and ensure equitable access to Digital Twins are vital. Moreover, sustainability should be a key consideration in 
the design and implementation of Digital Twins, aiming to minimize their environmental footprint and maximize their 
contribution to sustainable development goals. 
The advent of Digital Twins offers immense possibilities for innovation, optimization, and efficiency across numerous sectors. 
However, as with any transformative technology, it is imperative to navigate the ethical terrain with care. Addressing concerns 
related to privacy, intellectual property, ethical use, and social and environmental impacts is essential for the responsible 
development and deployment of Digital Twins. By fostering an ethical framework that emphasizes security, fairness, and 
sustainability, we can harness the full potential of Digital Twins while safeguarding against their risks and challenges. In doing 
so, we pave the way for a future where technology serves the greater good, enhancing our capabilities without compromising 
our values.

PRINCIPLES OF GOOD RESEARCH PRACTICES FOR THE AVANT PROJECT 
ENG, through AVANT project, is committed to upholding the highest standards of research integrity, as outlined in the 
European Code of Conduct for Research Integrity
1. These principles serve as a foundation for guiding our researchers in their 
work and in addressing the practical, ethical, and intellectual challenges inherent in research. By adhering to these principles, 
we ensure that our research is conducted with reliability, honesty, respect, and accountability, fostering a culture of excellence 
and trust within the scientific community and beyond. The principles defined in the European Code of Conduct for Research 
Integrity were anchored to the needs of the project: 
Reliability 
Ensuring Quality: In the AVANT project, reliability is paramount. This means designing robust research protocols, employing 
rigorous methodologies, and conducting thorough analyses. Every stage of the research process, from data collection to 
interpretation, must adhere to high standards to ensure the validity and reproducibility of results. 
Resource Utilization: Efficient and effective use of resources, including time, funding, and materials, is crucial. This involves 
careful planning and management to maximize the impact of the research while minimizing waste and inefficiencies.

2.3 
HUMAN RIGHTS COMPLIANCE IN AVANT 
The Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union, often referred to simply as “the Charter,” is a key legal instrument 
enshrining the personal, political, economic, civil, and social rights of individuals within the European Union. The AVANT 
project must be particularly mindful of the potential impact its AI-driven tools and technologies may have on the rights 
protected by the Charter, particularly in relation to human dignity (Article 1), freedoms (Articles 6-19), equality (Articles 20-
26), and justice (Articles 47-50). 
In AVANT, it is essential to ensure that human dignity and privacy (Articles 1 and 7) are respected. The design and use of 
these tools should include measures to protect the right to privacy, thus preventing any misuse of personal data or its 
transformation into a tool for unauthorized surveillance. Particular attention should be paid to data protection (Article 8), 
ensuring that personal data are processed securely and transparently, allowing individuals to exercise their rights over their 
own data. 
Non-discrimination (Article 21) is another key consideration for AVANT. AI systems and digital twins (DTs) developed under 
the project should be carefully designed to avoid any form of bias, ensuring they do not discriminate based on protected 
characteristics such as race, gender, or ethnic origin. Ensuring that systems are designed with fairness at their core helps to 
ensure both legal and ethical compliance, avoiding any form of disproportionate targeting of particular groups. 
In line with the principles outlined in the Charter, AVANT should include several additional safeguards to ensure the 
protection of fundamental rights. For instance, workers’ rights (Article 31) must be upheld, particularly in manufacturing and 
other sectors where digital twins might impact labour conditions. AVANT should ensure that the deployment of digital twins 
does not compromise workers’ rights and that fair working conditions are maintained. 
Consumer protection (Article 38) is also crucial. Ensuring that digital twins in smart cities and other domains protect consumer 
rights is essential. AVANT should implement measures to safeguard consumers from potential risks associated with digital 
twin technologies. In the health sector, digital twins must ensure access to high-quality healthcare services (Article 35). 
AVANT should focus on enhancing healthcare delivery while protecting patient rights and privacy. 
Environmental protection (Article 37) is important for digital twins in energy and smart cities to promote sustainable 
development. AVANT should ensure that its technologies contribute to environmental sustainability and do not harm the 
ecosystem. Additionally, ensuring access to services of general economic interest (Article 36) is vital. AVANT should work 
towards making these services more efficient and accessible through its technologies. 
Beyond these specific rights, AVANT should also consider broader aspects of human rights compliance. Transparency and 
accountability are essential; the processes and decisions made by AI systems and digital twins should be transparent, with 
clear documentation and communication about how data is collected, processed, and used. Accountability mechanisms 
should be in place to address any potential misuse or errors. 
Engaging with stakeholders, including the public, to raise awareness about the use of digital twins and AI technologies can 
help build trust and ensure that the technologies are aligned with societal values and expectations. Conducting regular 
human rights impact assessments throughout the lifecycle of the project helps identify and mitigate any potential adverse 
impacts on human rights. 
Applying a multi-stakeholder approach by involving various groups such as government bodies, private sector, civil society, 
and affected communities ensures diverse perspectives are considered and enhances the legitimacy of the project. 
Establishing independent oversight mechanisms to monitor compliance with human rights standards and providing 
accessible remedies for individuals whose rights may be adversely affected by the technologies are also crucial steps.

GDPR 
The AVANT project has the potential to operates within highly sensitive frameworks, building and deploying Digital Twins 
relying on AI-driven tools that could process personal data. Given the significant impact on individuals’ rights, adherence to 
the General Data Protection Regulation (EU) 2016/679, is essential. This regulatory framework ensures that all processing of 
personal data is lawful, fair, and transparent, while respecting the fundamental rights and freedoms of individuals across the 
European Union. 
In the context of data protection and privacy, the roles of data controller and data processor are crucial to understand and 
define clearly in any project involving personal data.  
A data controller is the entity that determines the purposes and means of processing personal data. Essentially, the data 
controller decides why and how personal data should be processed. For example, considering a city that wants to develops a 
digital twin to improve urban management, the city would collect the data from various sources (such as sensors, cameras, 
and user devices) to create a virtual model of the city. The city would also decide the purpose (e.g., improving traffic 
management, enhancing public safety) and the means (e.g., types of data collected, data analysis methods) of processing this 
data, making it the data controller. 
On the other hand, a data processor is an entity that processes personal data on behalf of the data controller. The data 
processor acts under the instructions of the data controller and does not have the authority to decide the purposes and 
means of processing. For instance, if the city outsources the development and maintenance of the digital twin to a 
technology company, this company would be the data processor. The technology company processes the data according to 
the city’s instructions without making independent decisions about the data. 
So, defining the roles of data controller and data processor in a project is essential for legal compliance, accountability, 
protection of data subject rights, risk management, and contractual clarity. This clear delineation helps ensure that personal 
data is handled responsibly and in accordance with applicable data protection laws, especially in complex projects like digital 
twins.

 E-PRIVACY REGULATION – PROPOSAL 
The European Commission has started a major modernisation process of the data protection framework over the past few 
years, which culminated in the adoption of the GDPR, as described in the previous sub-section. The ePrivacy legislation 
needed also to be adapted to align with these new rules. The European ePrivacy Regulation is an important amendment to 
the existing ePrivacy directive of 2002
3. The amendment is needed to cater for new technological and market developments, 
such as the current widespread use of Voice over IP, web-based email and messaging services, and the emergence of new 
techniques for tracking users’ online behavior. 
According to Article 1, Subject matter, the regulation lays down rules regarding the protection of fundamental rights and 
freedoms of natural persons in the provision and use of electronic communications services, and in particular, the rights to 
respect for private life and communications and the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal 
data. 
This Regulation also lays down rules regarding the protection of the fundamental rights and freedoms of legal persons in the 
provision and use of the electronic communications services, and particularly their rights to respect of communications. 
According to Article 2, Material Scope, this Regulation applies to: 
(a) the processing of electronic communications content and of electronic communications metadata carried out in 
connection with the provision and the use of electronic communications services; 
(b) end-users' terminal equipment information; 
(c) the offering of a publicly available directory of end-users of electronic communications services; 
(d) the sending of direct marketing communications to end-users. 
On February 2021, EU Member States agreed on a negotiating mandate for revised rules on the protection of privacy and 
confidentiality in the use of electronic communications services. These updated ‘ePrivacy’ rules will define cases in which 
service providers are allowed to process electronic communications data or have access to data stored on end-users’ devices. 
Under the Council mandate, the regulation will cover electronic communications content transmitted using publicly available 
services and networks, and metadata related to the communication. Metadata includes, for example, information on location 
and the time and recipient of communication. It is considered potentially as sensitive as the content. 
To ensure full protection of privacy rights and to promote a trusted and secure Internet of Things, the rules will also cover 
machine-to-machine data transmitted via a public network. 
The rules will apply when end-users are in the EU. This also covers cases where the processing takes place outside the EU or 
the service provider is established or located outside the EU. 
As a main rule, electronic communications data will be confidential. Any interference, including listening to, monitoring and 
processing of data by anyone other than the end-user will be prohibited, except when permitted by the ePrivacy regulation.

REGULATION ON THE FREE FLOW OF NON-PERSONAL DATA 
The Regulation on the free flow of non-personal data
4establishes a framework for the free flow of non-personal data within 
the European Union. The primary aim of this regulation is to ensure that electronic data, other than personal data, can be 
processed freely across the EU without unjustified restrictions. This regulation prohibits data localization requirements, which 
are rules that mandate data to be stored or processed within a specific geographical area. By removing these barriers, the 
regulation promotes data mobility, enhances the competitiveness of EU businesses, and supports the development of the 
data economy. It also ensures that the same principles of free movement that apply to goods and services within the EU are 
extended to non-personal data, fostering a more integrated and efficient digital single market. 
Together with the GDPR, the new Regulation on the free flow of non-personal data provides for a stable legal and business 
environment on data processing. This Regulation prevents EU countries from putting laws in place that unjustifiably force 
data to be held solely inside national territory. It is the first of its kind in the world. The new rules increase legal certainty and 
trust for businesses and make it easier for SMEs and start-ups to develop new innovative services, to make use of the best 
offers of data processing services in the internal market, and to expand business across borders. 
The free flow of non-personal data rules are in line with existing regulations for the free movement and portability of 
personal data in the EU. They: 
 
Ensure the free flow of data across borders: These rules set a framework for data storing and processing across 
the EU, preventing data localisation restrictions. Member States have to communicate any remaining or planned 
data localisation restrictions to the Commission, which in turn assesses if they are justifiable. The two Regulations 
function together to enable the free flow of any data – personal and non-personal – thus creating a common 
European space for data. In the case of a mixed dataset, the GDPR provision guaranteeing free flow of personal data 
will apply to the personal data part of the set, and the free flow of non-personal data principle will apply to the non-
personal part. 
 
Ensure data availability for regulatory control: Public authorities are able to access data for scrutiny and 
supervisory control wherever it is stored or processed in the EU. Member States may sanction users that do not 
provide upon request by a competent authority access to data stored in another Member State. 
 
Encourage the development of codes of conduct for cloud services to facilitate switching between cloud service 
providers. This allowed the market for cloud services to be more flexible and the data services in the EU more 
affordable. 
This regulation is particularly interesting for digital twin solutions, such as those developed under the AVANT project, as these 
solutions often involve the collection, processing, and analysis of large volumes of non-personal data. Digital twins, which are 
virtual replicas of physical entities, rely on real-time data to simulate and optimize processes across various sectors. The free 
flow of non-personal data is crucial for the effective functioning of digital twins, as it allows for seamless data integration and 
analysis across different locations and systems within the EU.

AI ACT – PROPOSAL 
The creation of AVANT solution involves the development, deployment, and use of artificial intelligence (AI)- based systems 
and techniques which may raise certain ethics and human rights concerns. For that reason, they must be built upon the key 
prerequisites of human agency and oversight, privacy and data governance, transparency, fairness, diversity and non-
discrimination, societal and environmental well-being and accountability, as well as in compliance with the European Proposal 
for a Regulation laying down harmonized rules on Artificial Intelligence. 
On 21 April 2021, a proposal for a Regulation laying down harmonized rules on Artificial Intelligence (Artificial Intelligence 
Act)
5 has been issued. The aim is to ensure the proper functioning of the single market by creating conditions for the 
development and use of secure, trustworthy and ethical AI in the Union. The specific objectives are: (1) to ensure that AI 
systems placed on the Union market and used are safe and respect the existing law on fundamental rights and Union values; 
(2) to ensure legal certainty to facilitate investment and innovation in AI; (3) to enhance governance and effective 
enforcement of existing law on fundamental rights and safety requirements applicable to AI systems; and (4) to facilitate the 
development of a single market for lawful, safe and trustworthy AI applications and prevent market fragmentation.  
The Proposal for Artificial Intelligence Act follows a risk-based approach and imposes specific obligations depending on the 
likelihood of the AI system to pose risks to fundamental rights and safety, differentiating between uses of AI that create an 
“unacceptable risk”, a “high risk” and “low or minimal risk”. 
The AVANT AI tools will have to be in compliance with the provided requirements and obligations once the Regulation will 
enter into force. Even if it is still in a draft version, it can be anticipated that, for instance, high-risk AI systems will have to 
comply with specific requirements, such as risk management system, human oversight, accuracy, robustness and 
cybersecurity, detailed documentation, the training, validation and testing must be subject to appropriate data governance 
and management practices and transparency in order to enable users to interpret the system’s output and use it 
appropriately.  
As soon as the AI Act will reach its final version, each AI based tools/solutions in AVANT will be assest in order to

3.5.1 
 NIS2 DIRECTIVE 
On 16 December 2020, the European Commission and the High Representative of the Union for Foreign Affairs and Security 
Policy presented a new EU Cybersecurity Strategy. 
The Cybersecurity Strategy for the Digital Decade aimed to respond to the cyber-related challenges posed by increasing 
digitalisation, the dependence on modern technologies and various complex threats. According to the document, cyberspace 
‘is increasingly exploited for political and ideological purposes and the EU ‘lacks collective situational awareness of cyber 
threats’ (pp.2-3). It thus describes the daunting task of improving the EU’s cybersecurity to safeguard fundamental rights and 
freedoms and to boost effective and systematic cooperation. 
The Strategy acknowledges cybersecurity as a multi-level issue, proposing a holistic approach. The text is divided into three 
areas of action: (1) resilience, technological sovereignty and leadership; (2) building operational capacity to prevent, deter and 
respond; and (3) advancing a global and open cyberspace. All these areas contain concrete proposals for deploying 
instruments regarding regulatory, investment and policy initiatives. It also recognizes the critical nature of the EU institutions, 
bodies and agencies that carry a greater risk of becoming targets of cyber-attacks. 
As highlighted in the Strategy, EU rules on the security of Network and Information Systems (NIS) are at the core of the 
Single Market for cybersecurity and therefore the Commission proposed to reform these rules under a revised NIS Directive 
to increase the level of cyber resilience of all relevant sectors, public and private, that perform an important function for the 
economy and society. The review was necessary to reduce inconsistencies across the internal market by aligning scope, 
security and incident reporting requirements, national supervision and enforcement and the capabilities of competent 
authorities. 
The NIS2 Directive is the EU-wide legislation on cybersecurity. It provides legal measures to boost the overall level of 
cybersecurity in the EU. The EU cybersecurity rules introduced in 2016 were updated by the NIS2 Directive that will repeal the 
previous one with effect from 18 October 2024. It modernized the existing legal framework to keep up with increased 
digitisation and an evolving cybersecurity threat landscape. By expanding the scope of the cybersecurity rules to new sectors 
and entities, it further improves the resilience and incident response capacities of public and private entities, competent 
authorities and the EU as a whole. 
The NIS 2 Directive expands the scope of the previous NIS Directive by adding new sectors based on their criticality for the 
economy and society, and by introducing a clear size cap – meaning that all medium and large companies in selected 
sectors are also included in the scope. Some flexibility is left for Member States to identify smaller entities with a high 
security risk profile. The NIS 2 Directive also eliminates the distinction between operators of essential services and digital 
service providers. Instead, entities are classified as either essential entities (referred to in Annex I) or important entities 
(referred to in Annex II) and subjected to different supervisory regimes. A registry of all essential and important entities in the 
EU are being created and maintained by ENISA. 
Thus, any essential or important entity listed in Annex I or Annex II which meets the EU level thresholds for medium or large 
enterprises, or which are designated by Member States irrespective of their size must take appropriate and proportionate 
technical, operational and organisational measures to manage the risks posed to the security of network and information 
systems which those entities use for their operations or for the provision of their services, and to prevent or minimise the 
impact of incidents on recipients of their services and on other services. They must implement security risk management 
practices that are based on an all-hazards approach, which aims to protect network and information systems and the physical 
environment of those systems from incident.

CYBERSECURITY ACT 
In order to strengthen the EU’s resilience to cyber-attacks, as well as deterrence thereof and defenses against such attacks, 
the European Commission proposed a new policy package in 2017
12. The centerpiece of this policy package was a proposal to 
reform the European Agency for Network and Information Security (ENISA) into an EU Cybersecurity Agency. The goal of this 
Agency is to “improve the EU's preparedness to react by organizing yearly pan-European cybersecurity exercises and by 
ensuring better sharing of threat intelligence and knowledge through the setting up of Information Sharing and Analyses 
Centres”, as well as to help Member States with the implementation of the NIS Directive. At the same time, the Agency would 
“help put in place and implement the EU-wide certification framework that the Commission is proposing to ensure that 
products and services are cyber secure”.
13 
Article 1 designates ENISA as the European Union Agency for Cybersecurity. Cybersecurity is to be understood as “the 
activities necessary to protect network and information systems, the users of such systems, and other persons affected by 
cyber threats”.15 Most of the other terms defined in the EU Cybersecurity Act follow the definitions from the NIS Directive. 
Article 3 provides a mandate to ENISA as the European Union Agency for Cybersecurity to achieve a high common level of 
cybersecurity across the Union, while article 4 defines the objectives of the Agency. The Agency will assist and advise on 
Union policy in this field, as well as help Member States in implementing those policies.16 The Agency will also support 
capacity-building (article 6), operational cooperation at the Union level (article 7), and cybersecurity certification and 
standardization (article 8). Furthermore, the Agency will develop and share knowledge in this field (article 9), raise public 
awareness and provide education (article 10), and advice on and participate in research and innovation (article 11). Overall, 
these provisions are fairly broad and general. They mainly serve to establish the broadest possible mandate for the Agency in 
the field of cybersecurity. For the purposes of the AVANT project, the mandate is less directly relevant – while the eventual 
live implementation of AVANT outputs may be of interest to ENISA under its general mandate, there are no immediate 
implications in the short term.  
Of greater interest is Title III of the Act, which provides for a new, EU level cybersecurity certification framework. This 
framework aims to “improve the conditions for the functioning of the internal market by increasing the level of cybersecurity 
within the Union and enabling a harmonised approach at Union level to cybersecurity certification schemes, with a view to 
creating a digital single market for ICT products, ICT services and ICT processes” (article 46). It establishes European 
cybersecurity certification schemes to attest that ICT products, processes and services evaluated in accordance with such 
schemes comply with specified security requirements aiming to protect the availability, authenticity, integrity or 
confidentiality of stored or transmitted or processed data or the functions or services offered by, or accessible via, those 
products, processes, and services throughout their life cycle. The European Commission will establish a work program to 
identify strategic priorities for future European Cybersecurity Certification schemes (article 47). The Agency may also be 
requested to draft a candidate European cybersecurity certification scheme (article 49).  
Article 51 defines the security objectives of the certification schemes. They must prevent unauthorized access to, or disclosure 
or removal of data. They must allow to check who accessed which data, services or functions. They must keep in mind the 
principles of security by design and security by default. Certification schemes can provide different assurance levels, 
commensurate with the level of risk associated with a process, product or service (article 52). A basic assurance level aims to 
minimize known basic risks. A substantial security level assures minimization of known risks. A high security level shows 
minimization of risks of state-of-the-art cyberattacks. Cybersecurity certification schemes may allow for conformity self-

DATA GOVERNANCE ACT 
A key pillar of the European strategy for data, the Data Governance Act seeks to increase trust in data sharing, strengthen 
mechanisms to increase data availability and overcome technical obstacles to the reuse of data. The Data Governance Act also 
supports the setup and development of Common European Data Spaces in strategic domains, involving both private and 
public players, in sectors such as health, environment, energy, agriculture, mobility, finance, manufacturing, public 
administration and skills. The Data Governance Act (DGA) entered into force on 23 June 2022 and, following a 15 month 
grace period, is now applicable since September 2023. 
The horizontal regulation is binding in its entirety and directly applicable in all Member States. The primary purpose of the 
DGA is to increase trust in data intermediaries, i.e., organizers of data sharing such as data marketplaces, and thus increase 
the availability of data (recitals 5, 32 DGA). The current reluctance of companies to use data intermediation services and share 
their data with other companies is, in the Commission’s view, primarily caused by trust deficits in data markets. By helping to 
establish the necessary trust in data intermediaries, the DGA supports these intermediaries in gaining a larger user base. This 
aims to support a flourishing data exchange in the EU. In addition, the DGA is intended to prevent potentially anti-
competitive behavior of data intermediation service providers such as self-preferences or unequal treatment (recital 33 DGA).

The DGA is intended to facilitate the enhanced re-use of data in the possession of public bodies that are protected by other 
rights. These special protection rights include commercial and statistical confidentiality, the protection of intellectual property 
of third parties and the protection of personal data (Art. 3 para. 1 DGA). The EU legislator stipulates certain requirements for 
the further use of personal data. Such data must either be anonymised, modified or made accessible only within a secure 
processing environment as defined in Art. 2 No. 20 DGA (Art. 5 para. 3 DGA). Any conditions for re-use must be non-
discriminatory, transparent, proportionate and objectively justified (Art. 5 para. 2 DGA). However, the DGA does not oblige 
public sector bodies to allow the re-use of data (Art. 1 para. 2 DGA). In particular, it does not grant users a right to access 
data.  
To help users find their way through the “data and information jungle,” the DGA demands Member States to establish central 
information points that provide easy access to relevant information. Furthermore, Member States are required to designate 
one or more competent bodies to assist public authorities in fulfilling their new responsibilities (Art. 7 DGA). For instance, the 
public sector bodies should receive support in ensuring data processing that preserves privacy, confidentiality, integrity and 
accessibility of data. The core concern of the DGA is to ensure that data is made available quickly. The public sector bodies (if 
applicable also the competent bodies according to Art. 7 DGA) must therefore decide on a request for further data use within 
2 months (Art. 9 para. 1 DGA). The DGA also aims at supporting data altruism, i.e., “the voluntary sharing of data on the basis 
of the consent of data subjects to process personal data pertaining to them, or permissions of data holders to allow the use 
of their non-personal data without seeking or receiving a reward [. . . ] for objectives of general interest as provided for in 
national law” (Art. 2 No. 16 DGA). The legal definition gives examples of the public purposes which include healthcare, 
combating climate change, improving mobility, public policy making or scientific research purposes in the general interest. 
In addition to the provisions of Art. 5, the DGA mainly establishes basic organizational conditions: these include, for example, 
regulations for a public register on recognized data altruistic organizations (Art. 17 et seq. DGA), transparency requirements 
(Art. 20 DGA), specific requirements to safeguard rights and interests of data subjects and data holders (Art. 21 DGA), 
provisions for a so-called rulebook (Art. 22 DGA) as well as regulations for monitoring by the competent authorities (Art. 23 et 
seq. DGA). Finally, Art. 25 DGA contains requirements for a “European data altruism consent form.” The EU legislator hopes 
that the standardized form will make it easier to collect data on the basis of data altruism (Art. 25 para. 1 DGA). To allow its 
use in certain sectors and for different purposes, the consent form is intended to be modular (Art. 25 para. 2 DGA). The 
Commission is empowered to adopt implementing acts specifying the details. 
Moreover, the DGA establishes a notification and monitoring framework for so-called data intermediation service providers 
(Art. 2 No. 11 DGA). These aim to establish business relationships to share data between an undetermined number of data 
subjects or data owners (Art. 2 No. 7, 8 DGA) on the one hand and data users (Art. 2 No. 9 DGA) on the other. Data 
intermediation service providers are generally obliged to be registered by the competent authority of the Member State (Art. 
11 para. 1 DGA). In addition, anyone offering data intermediation services must fulfill several conditions (Art. 12 DGA), in 
particular the principle of purpose limitation: the provider is not allowed to use the data for which it provides services for 
purposes other than to put them at the disposal for data users (Art. 12 lit. a DGA). Changes of purpose are thus excluded in 
principle. Metadata may only be used to further develop the service (Art. 12 lit. c DGA). The intermediary is also obliged to 
provide for appropriate technical, legal and organizational measures to prevent the unlawful transfer of or access to non-
personal data (Art. 12 lit. j DGA). In addition, the provider must ensure an adequate level of security for the storage, 
processing and transmission of non-personal data and especially of sensitive information (Art. 12 lit. l DGA). Monitoring 
compliance with the requirements on data intermediary services according to Chapter III (Art. 10-14) of the DGA ex post is the 
responsibility of the competent authority (Art. 14 para. 1 DGA) (Spindler, 2021). If the authority finds an infringement, it can 
demand within a reasonable period of time (or immediately in the case of serious violations) that the provider ceases the 
infringement. In addition, the authority can, for example, impose dissuasive financial penalties (Art. 14 para. 4 lit. a DGA).

an expert group composed of, among others, representatives of certain Member State authorities, the European Data 
Protection Board (EDPB), the European Data Protection Supervisor (EDPS), the European Union Agency for Cybersecruity

DATA ACT – PROPOSAL 
In February 2022, the Commission presented a proposal for a Regulation on harmonized rules on fair access to and use of 
data, also known as the Data Act. It is a key pillar of the European Data Strategy. The DA is intended to supplement the 
provisions of the DGA on the basis of Article 114 TFEU in order to create a single market for data that grants consumers more 
rights, strengthens the negotiating position of smaller companies and de-monopolizes data. On the normative foundation of 
the DGA, the DA specifies the preconditions for specific data use or value creation. Another aim of the DA is to strike a 
balance between the needs of the digital economy and a fair and secure data use. Both aspects are important in order to 
implement innovations such as DTs. 
Chapters I–IV of the draft regulation focus on rules for governing data access between data owners, users and third parties as 
data recipients. Chapter V specifies the access rights of public authorities. Chapters VI, VII, IIX DA Proposal include regulations 
on the portability of data between processing services and guarantees the protection of non-personal data against unlawful 
transfers to or state access from third countries. 
Besides, interoperability requirements for data are stipulated. The regulations are highly relevant for the functioning of the 
single data market. In particular, in order to promote the European data market and reduce existing hurdles, the DA intends 
to create a legally secure framework for data transfers. It seeks to cushion the effects of asymmetric market positions: small 
and medium sized enterprises (SMEs) are to be protected by the development and provision of – optionally usable – fair 
model contractual terms and conditions in the value-creating transfer of data (Art. 34 DA Proposal). 
The draft DA grants public bodies the right to privileged access to data if they can prove an “exceptional need,” e.g., due to a 
natural disaster (Art. 14 para. 1 DA Proposal). This may concern not only company data, but also personal data. If necessary, 
this data must be pseudonymised in accordance with Art. 4 No. 5 GDPR before government agencies receive them. In order 
to facilitate switching between data processing services and to counteract dominant market positions of individual 
companies, in particular to prevent so-called “lock-in effects,” the draft DA stipulates interoperability requirements (Art. 28). 
This is consistent with the protection of the individual data user by access rights to data: the data holder should make the 
data available to the user of a networked product or related service without delay, free of charge and, where appropriate, 
continuously and in real time (Art. 4 para. 1). Moreover, the idea of Art. 3 para. 1 DA Proposal is “access by design”: products 
are to be manufactured in a way that the data generated during their use is easily and securely accessible. For projects that 
rely on data, such as DTs, this regulation is quite beneficial. 
The application scope of the proposed DA includes personal and non-personal data. Thus, the protection of privacy may be 
affected by the DA. In principle, Art. 1 No. 3 and the recitals of the DA Proposal state that the Regulation merely complements 
Union law on data protection and privacy, in particular the GDPR and the Directive on privacy and electronic communications 
2002/58/EC. No provision of the DA shall be applied or interpreted in a way that weakens or restricts the right to protection 
of personal data or the right to privacy and confidentiality of communications. As a result, in cases in which the DA applies to 
personal data, provisions of the DA and the GDPR must be complied with.
